01

Executive summary

WhyThis is software that gives engineering teams back the reasoning behind their own codebase. It connects to a company's GitHub, Slack, and Jira, indexes the commits, pull requests, messages, and tickets that make up its decision history, and answers plain-language questions — "why does calculateTax() work this way?" — with the specific commit, PR, and approver behind that decision, not a plausible-sounding guess.

The product is built and functional: a multi-tenant backend (each client's data and credentials fully isolated), retrieval over embedded decision history, a citation-forced answer model, a web dashboard, and a Slack slash command. It is pre-revenue, and the near-term plan is a small, free pilot program with 3–5 engineering teams to validate answer quality and willingness to pay before any wider go-to-market spend.

The target customer is narrow and deliberate: software companies with 20–200 people and 10–50 engineers — large enough that institutional knowledge routinely walks out the door, small enough that a single champion (a CTO or VP Engineering) can approve the purchase without a procurement process.

02

The problem

Companies remember what happened. They lose why. A developer finds an unusual conditional, a disabled retry, a hardcoded exception for one client — and the reasoning behind it lives nowhere durable: a Slack thread from eighteen months ago, in the memory of an engineer who has since left, or nowhere at all beyond a PR description that just says "fix bug."

The standard response today is to ask around and wait, or to skip asking and guess. Both are expensive in a way that's easy to underweight because it happens in small, constant increments rather than one visible cost: minutes lost per question, multiplied across a team, multiplied across every week a company operates without a durable record of its own reasoning.

03

The product

WhyThis connects to a client's GitHub repository, Slack workspace, and Jira project using read-only, revocable credentials. It indexes commits, pull request titles and descriptions, Slack messages, and Jira tickets — embedding each one and linking related items together, so a PR, its Slack discussion, and its Jira ticket resolve as a single answer instead of three separate searches.

When a question is asked — from the dashboard or via a Slack /why-this command — the system retrieves the most relevant indexed items and asks a language model to synthesize a direct answer, deliberately instructed to cite its sources and to say plainly when the indexed history doesn't contain a clear answer.

04

How it works

01

Connect

A read-only GitHub token, a Slack bot invited to a few channels, a Jira project. No new tool for engineers to learn.

02

Index

Commits, PRs, messages, and tickets are embedded and cross-referenced into one searchable decision history, scoped privately to that client alone.

03

Ask

A question in the dashboard or /why-this in Slack returns a sourced answer — or an honest "the history doesn't say" — in seconds.

05

Market & target customer

The intended customer is software companies with roughly 20–200 employees and 10–50 engineers: large enough that engineers routinely touch code they didn't write and can't easily ask its author, small enough that a CTO or VP Engineering can approve a purchase directly. Distribution leans on channels where that buyer is already reachable directly — CTO-focused LinkedIn outreach, YC and startup communities, engineering-leadership Slack groups, and warm referrals.

Illustrative unit economics — not a forecast A 30-engineer client at $15/user/month is $450/month (above the $300 minimum) — roughly $5,400/year per client. Fifty such clients would be on the order of $270,000 in annual recurring revenue. These figures size the model, not a claim about clients signed to date.

07

Positioning

The wedge is deliberately narrow: answering "why does this exist?" with a citation. Everything below is a conscious choice not to build it — each is a larger, more crowded, more capital-intensive product than the problem currently calls for.

This is not

  • A general company chatbot
  • A Notion or wiki competitor
  • A full organizational knowledge graph
  • Meeting transcription or AI notetaking
  • Employee monitoring or activity scoring

This is

  • One question, answered with a citation
  • Read-only, revocable access — nothing autonomous
  • Isolated per client, by design
  • Honest when the history doesn't have an answer
  • Usable without changing how engineers already work
